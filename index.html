I build simulations that help organizations decide which cyber defenses to invest in. Good defensive tools already exist. The hard part is choosing the combination that fits one organization's people, systems, and budget, so I model attackers, defenders, and employees together and judge a defense by the damage the organization actually sustains.

Research

My thesis work, Human-Centric Simulation for Organizational Cyber Defense, connects three questions: how real attacks unfold, which defenses are worth their cost, and how the people inside an organization change the outcome.

Attack Flow Networks

Real attacks are chains of techniques, not isolated events. I built the Cyber Attack Flow Intelligence Network from 1,031 public threat reports covering 170 MITRE ATT&CK groups and 50 campaigns. It records which technique tends to follow which, so simulated attackers take routes seen in real incidents instead of hand-written scenarios.

Defense Optimization

Given that network, which defenses cut the attacker off at the lowest cost? I answer this with minimum vertex cuts and budget-constrained interdiction, and I test the resulting plans against attackers who adapt and persist. The best plan depends on the budget: with ample funds, technical controls that disrupt post-intrusion activity; with tight ones, employee training and blocking initial malware execution.

Human-Centric Simulation

OSIRIS is an agent-based model of an organization whose employees differ in phishing susceptibility, fatigue, emotion, and social ties. It shows how people change what a defense delivers. In one study, the most accurate intrusion detector allowed more data theft than three less accurate ones, because it never monitored the techniques on the attacker's path.
